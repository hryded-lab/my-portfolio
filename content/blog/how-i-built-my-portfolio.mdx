I'm a manufacturing engineering student, not a CS student. I don't have semesters of algorithms and data structures behind me, just curiosity, a laptop, and an interest in building things.

So when I decided I needed a portfolio, I didn't do it the traditional way. I built it by vibe coding, using AI (Claude, mostly) to write large chunks of the code while I directed, reviewed, debugged, and shaped it into what I wanted.

Here's what that taught me.

## What vibe coding actually means

It's not letting the AI do everything and taking credit. That produces garbage. It's closer to being an architect who can't lay every brick themselves. You need to understand the structure, make the decisions, and know when something is wrong even if you couldn't have built it from scratch.

In practice that meant I knew I wanted a Windows XP desktop. I knew I wanted a Clippy-inspired assistant. I knew I wanted a command prompt with easter eggs. Turning those into working code took understanding what I was asking for, reviewing the output critically, catching bugs, and making judgment calls constantly.

## Prompting forced me to think more precisely

This surprised me. To get a good result you have to describe what you want clearly enough that an AI can implement it, which means understanding the problem, the constraints, and the edge cases. You can't be vague and get something good back.

I picked up more about React hooks, state management, and streaming APIs by trying to get them right through prompting than I would have from reading documentation.

## The parts it couldn't do

- Design decisions. AI doesn't know what looks right for your personality.
- Debugging. When something breaks subtly you need to understand the system well enough to diagnose it. AI helps, but you lead the investigation.
- Content. The words, the descriptions, the voice. No prompt fixes shallow content.
- Taste. Knowing when something is good enough versus when it needs more work.

## The honest part

I started this to get away from the boredom of the semester, make something worth showing, and figure out how to actually work with AI tools. I didn't expect it to get as involved as it did.

It ended up with a boot sequence, a lock screen, draggable windows, a streaming assistant, a working command prompt, and a separate phone OS for mobile. Every feature came out of a conversation: me directing, AI implementing, me revising.

Does that count as building it? I think so. The ideas, the decisions, and the judgment calls were mine. The AI was a fast pair programmer writing code I'd have taken far longer to write alone.

## If you want to try this

Start with a clear picture of what you want. The clearer your idea, the better the AI can help you get there. Vague in, generic out.

Review everything. Don't ship code you don't understand.

And build something you actually want to exist, not a template with your name on it.
